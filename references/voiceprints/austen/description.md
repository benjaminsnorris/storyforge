# The described voice: Jane Austen

## What the voice does

- Narration renders a character's opinion in that character's own words without quotation marks, so the verdict sounds like the narrator's until it turns.
- Leans heavily on negation and litotes, praising or blaming by denying the opposite.
- Prefers abstract Latinate nouns for feelings and conduct over physical description.
- Builds long sentences out of semicolon-joined clauses that each add a fresh qualification.
- Marks spoken stress with italics on single pronouns or small words.
- Gives each speaker a recognisable verbal tic, such as stock phrases, exclamations or dash-broken gushing.
- Tags dialogue with varied speech verbs plus a manner adverb or phrase.
- Lists money, income and fortune as exact figures alongside character traits.
- Ends a long passage of report with a short, flat sentence that undercuts it.

## What it never does

- Never uses contractions in narration, and almost never in the speech of educated characters.
- Almost never describes landscape, weather or objects in sensory detail for their own sake.
- Never uses colons or parentheses as ordinary narrative punctuation.
- Never lets the narrator address the reader as "you" or step forward as "I".

## Its stance toward the reader

- Takes the reader into confidence with dry general verdicts on human conduct, delivered as settled fact.
- Trusts the reader to catch irony without signalling it, leaving self-condemning speeches unanswered.
- Shifts freely between a cool outside summary and one character's private reasoning within a paragraph.

## Its humour

- The humour is deadpan anticlimax, where a sentence builds a serious frame and then drops in a trivial particular.
- Characters score off each other with paradox and symmetrical reversals in dialogue.
- Exaggeration is placed in a character's mouth and allowed to run until it exposes itself.

## Its rhythm

- Long narrative paragraphs alternate with runs of very short dialogue lines, many of them one sentence.
- Sentences often grow by triads and balanced pairs, three parallel items or an antithetical "not this but that."
- Inside excited speech, dashes break sentences into breathless fragments.
